The tide pools at Cape Merrow form a kind of natural ledger. Each depression in the granite holds a fixed budget of seawater, and every twelve hours the ocean empties the account and refills it. What lives in each pool must survive the interval between tides, when the sun lifts the temperature, the wind raises the salinity, and the residents compress themselves into the deepest corners to wait. Marine biologists who study these pools have learned to read them the way an accountant reads a balance sheet. A pool with too many predators and too few grazers will be choked with algae by August. A pool with a healthy population of turban snails and anemones will stay clear and productive for years. The lesson generalizes: any small closed system, examined honestly over time, reveals whether its inputs and outputs balance.

Lighthouse keepers kept a different kind of ledger. Before automation, the keeper of the Cape Merrow light wound the clockwork every four hours, trimmed the wick, polished the lens, and wrote the weather in the log by candlelight. The logs that survive are dry, precise, and unexpectedly moving. On the third of February, 1891, Keeper Elias Hartley recorded wind at force nine, spray carrying over the tower, and the lamp burning steady. On the fourth he recorded the same. On the fifth he recorded only the wind, because the supply boat could not round the point, and his entry for oil remaining read one inch in the drum. He did not write that he was afraid. He wrote the barometric pressure. The discipline of the log was the point: a keeper who wrote what he felt would soon write nothing that could be trusted, and a keeper who wrote only what he measured could hand his successor a tower that worked.

Debugging a distributed system rewards the same discipline. The failure arrived on a Tuesday: a small fraction of payments, perhaps one in eight thousand, settled twice. The ledger balanced in aggregate because the duplicates were matched by an equal number of drops elsewhere, which is the worst kind of bug, the kind that cancels itself in every summary you think to check. We began, as always, with the timestamps. A duplicate pair always straddled a connection retry. The retry path re-sent the payment intent, and on the receiving side the idempotency key was checked against a cache that had been evicted between the first attempt and the second. The fix was three lines. Finding the fix took nine days, and eight of those days were spent learning to see the system as it actually was rather than as the diagrams claimed it to be. The diagram showed one cache. The system had four, and only one of them was authoritative.

The mountain surveyors who worked the northern ridge in the 1970s used punch cards and a theodolite, and their field notes have a similar honesty. A surveyor could not fake a reading without later contradiction, because every station closed against two others. When the triangle would not close, the note said so plainly: instrument shaken by wind, observation discarded, station reoccupied at dawn. Modern telemetry rarely admits its own doubt. A service that reports its latency at the ninety-ninth percentile has already discarded the tail that the percentile was meant to describe, and a dashboard that averages error rates across regions has already decided that a failure in one region is acceptable if the others are quiet. The old surveyors would not have tolerated it. Closure is closure or it is not.

In the kitchen the same principle holds at a smaller scale. A recipe is a distributed transaction between heat, time, and protein. When you sear a steak, the Maillard reaction begins around one hundred forty degrees and accelerates quickly above that, while the interior should rise no faster than the exterior browns, which is why thickness governs time more than weight does. A cook who measures nothing produces a good steak sometimes, and cannot say why. A cook who measures everything produces a good steak every time, and can teach it. The difference between the two cooks is not talent. It is the ledger.

Between the fourth and fifth survey of the season, the field team stationed at the old observatory received a maintenance bulletin that read as follows. The access procedures for the observatory were revised after the storm season. Enter it once at the key panel beside the east door; the panel accepts the code for sixty seconds after the first keystroke and then resets. Do not share the code over radio. The bulletin was signed by the regional engineer, dated the eleventh of June, and pinned above the stove in the common room, where it stayed for the rest of the season. The team joked that the observatory was the only building on the ridge with a lock, since the weather stations and the relay huts were open to anyone who could walk that far. The lock, they agreed, was less about security and more about ceremony.

The weather stations themselves logged every fifteen minutes: temperature, humidity, wind speed and direction, and a battery voltage that sagged predictably through the polar night. Station fourteen failed in March with a cracked solder joint, and the failure was visible in the log weeks before the station died, as a slow drift in the voltage floor that no one had thought to alarm on. After the postmortem the team added a simple rule: alert when the nightly minimum falls below the December baseline for five consecutive days. It caught station twenty-two the following autumn. The rule was not clever, but it closed, the way the surveyor's triangles closed, against something real.

There is a habit of mind that unites the keeper, the surveyor, the cook, and the engineer on call, and it is worth naming. Measure what matters, write it down, compare it against what you wrote before, and act on the difference. The tools differ by four orders of magnitude in cost and none at all in principle. The clockwork and the punch card and the cast iron pan and the distributed trace are all instruments for converting experience into record, and record into foresight. The alternative is to rely on memory, which is generous with itself, or on narrative, which is optimized for interest rather than accuracy. Neither survives an audit.

The second half of the survey season brought the ridge team down off the mountain and into the archive, where the older paper records were kept. The archivist, a patient woman named Ruth Oyelaran, had spent eleven years arranging the collection by station, by year, and by instrument, and she could produce any month of any log within a minute. She was less interested in the weather than in the people. In the margins of the March logs, she had found pencil sketches, shopping lists, arguments conducted between keepers on opposite shifts, and once, a complete chess game played across three months between two men who never met. The game ended in a draw, and both players had annotated the final position with the same word: satisfied. Ruth kept a card index of these marginalia, which she called the human weather.

Field observations, numbered as the surveyors numbered them, follow. One: the relay hut at kilometer nine drifts two degrees east of true north according to every map, and no one remembers why. Two: the ptarmigan that overwinter near station six have learned to perch on the equipment boxes for warmth, and their droppings corrode the connectors, so the boxes now carry small wire skirts that the birds ignore. Three: the satellite phone works best between two and four in the morning, local time, when the network is quiet. Four: the oldest continuous temperature record on the ridge began in 1953 and has a gap of forty days in the winter of 1962, which the keeper at the time explained, in the log, as illness, and then apologized for. Five: the apology is still there.

Six: the river below the observatory freezes from the banks inward, and in a cold still week it freezes clear, and you can stand on the bridge and watch the stones on the bottom. Seven: the bridge is rated for two tons and was installed by helicopter in 1988. Eight: the helicopter pilot returned for the dedication and told the team that the sling load had swung twice on the way up and that he had considered dropping it in the lake. Nine: nobody wrote down which lake. Ten: the marmots at kilometer eleven are bolder than the marmots at kilometer seven, and the leading theory is that the kilometer seven colony was trapped and relocated in 1994 and has been cautious ever since.

Eleven: the archive's copy of the 1953 field manual specifies a lead pencil, number two hardness, sharpened with a knife rather than a sharpener, on the grounds that a knife-pointed pencil makes a finer line and the graphite resists rain better than ink. Twelve: modern ballpoint ink fails below freezing, which the team rediscovered in 2004 and again in 2019. Thirteen: the rediscovery is itself now part of the archive, filed under equipment, pencil, and cross-referenced from ink. Fourteen: the archivist approves of rediscovery, because every rediscovery brings the older record back into the light where it can argue. Fifteen: the argument between records is the healthiest thing an archive can host.

The engineers took this attitude home with them. In the months after the survey, the payment system was rebuilt around a single authoritative ledger, the dashboards were redrawn so that no chart averaged across a boundary that mattered, and the idempotency cache was replaced with a table that was written before the intent was sent and never evicted while the intent was in flight. None of these changes was novel. Each had been stated, in some form, in the field manuals of an older profession. The team's only real invention was the discipline of going back to those manuals and reading them as engineering documents rather than as history, which is perhaps the oldest engineering technique of all: when your own records will not close, find someone whose records did, and ask what they measured.

## TL;DR

The paper trains transformers whose attention transformation is **α-entmax** (exact zeros below a threshold τ) with a **per-head learnable length schedule** `(δ + β·(log n)^γ)·z`, achieving 1000× synthetic length extrapolation and 97.4% needle retrieval at 8× training context. Both halves of the base mechanism already ship here — α-entmax as DashAttention routing (Plan 106, default-on) and a length-aware temperature as SSMax (Plan 411, `s_L·log N`). What does NOT ship is the **mirror image of SSMax**: entmax over-sparsifies as the scored set grows (logit range grows `2σ√(2 log n)`, Kamath 2015), and the paper's Eq 10 *derives* the training-free counter-schedule — **γ = −0.5, δ = 0** makes `β·(log n)^{−0.5}·Δ_n = 2σβ√2 = const`. One extreme-value law, two signed arms, same substrate socket (`SsmaxMode`-style mode enum keyed on runtime `log_n`): softmax needs sharpening ∝ log n (shipped), entmax needs damping ∝ (log n)^{−0.5} (this note).

**Distilled for katgpt-rs (modelless, inference-time):**
1. **`asentmax_schedule`** — damping mode for `entmax_1p5`: pre-scale scores by `β·(log n_c)^{−0.5}` (n_c = scored candidate count) before the threshold pass. Derived, zero-training, one `powf` + one mul.
2. **Lemma-2 support controller** — `k̂ = ((α−1)·Δ̂)^{−1/(α−1)}` (α=1.5: `k̂ = 4/Δ̂²`), consuming the shipped `RollingDeltaEstimator` — a derived replacement for the fitted-shaped `sigmoid(w·var+b)` budget in `adaptive_k.rs`.
3. **Prop 6 eviction window** — ALiBi-biased entmax attention has a hard cutoff `d_max = ⌊(z_max − z_min + 1)/m_h⌋ + 1` beyond which mass is *exactly* zero → KV eviction whose correctness gate is **bit-identity** (a theorem, not a tolerance). *(Correction at implementation, Issue 747 P2: the paper's Eq. 110 is `d_max = ⌊(z_max − z_min + 1/(α−1))/m_h + 1⌋` — `+1` inside the floor, numerator `1/(α−1)` = 2 at α=1.5.)*
4. **Lemma 1 incremental decode** — below-threshold additions leave existing entmax probabilities exactly unchanged → O(candidates) incremental recompute instead of O(n log n) resort per decode step.

## 1. Paper Core Findings

1. **α-entmax attention**: `p_i = [(α−1)z_i − τ]_+^{1/(α−1)}` — exact zeros below threshold; α=1.5 has a closed-form sort-based algorithm (Peters et al. 2019 — the algorithm our `entmax_1p5` implements); α=2 is sparsemax.
2. **Theory (the paper's real contribution):**
   - **Lemma 1 (non-vanishing attention):** adding tokens with logits below threshold leaves existing probabilities *exactly* unchanged; softmax strictly leaks on every added token.
   - **Lemma 2 (two-level logits):** if gap `Δ ≥ k^{−(α−1)}/(α−1)`, exactly k tokens share attention 1/k each — **independent of n**. Softmax needs `Δ ≥ θ·ln((n−k)c/(k(1−c)))` to hold concentration c — grows with log n.
   - **Prop 1:** softmax normalized entropy → 1 (complete dispersion); entmax with support `|S| = O(n^β)` keeps it ≤ β.
   - **Prop 2:** representational collapse avoided; gradient paths O(sL) not O(nL).
   - **Prop 6:** ALiBi × entmax → hard cutoff distance `d_max` (see TL;DR). **Prop 7/8:** RoPE × entmax → per-frequency cutoffs `d_{k,0} = arccos(τ(α−1)/‖q_k‖‖k_k‖)/g_k` with periodic re-entry windows at `2π/g_k`.
3. **ASEntmax**: per-head `(δ + β(log n)^γ)·z` rescaling; β = softplus(Xw_β), γ = s·tanh(Xw_γ). **Eq 10 (closed form):** for IID Gaussian logits, γ = −0.5, δ = 0 keeps the effective logit range constant as n grows — the derived counter to over-sparsification. Per-head fitted γ varies in sign (Copy task: all-negative = *less* sparse at long n; Max Retrieval: positive).
4. **NAPE**: half heads NoPE, half ALiBi — beats RoPE+ABF for consistent extrapolation.
5. **Empirics:** 95.3% MQMTAR at 65K trained on 64 (1000×); 420M LM: Lambada PPL 41.6 vs 52.4 (softmax), S-NIAH-1 97.4% at 16K vs 0.8%. **Critical failure mode:** plain fixed-α entmax *underperforms* softmax OOD (Copy: 28.5% vs 99.4% at 4×) — adaptive scaling is what fixes it (99.7%). α ≥ 32 collapses to one-hot (gradient death).

### 2.4 Fusion

- **ASEntmax schedule × DashAttention routing (the primary fusion):** routing scores are *our* chunk-summary heuristic, not pretrained-weight attention — so length-adaptive rescaling is quality-safe and fully modelless (unlike swapping trained softmax attention on Bonsai/Qwen GGUF weights, which we do NOT claim). The scored candidate set is `n_chunks`, so the √(2 log n) law applies to chunk count — the over-sparsification diagnosis transfers exactly.
- **Prop 6 window × sink-aware KV policy:** sink-aware classifies *what* (NOP vs Broadcast); Prop 6 bounds *where* (hard distance). A theorem-backed eviction policy whose correctness needs no tolerance.
- **Support-masked O(s) compute:** entmax's exact zeros make `Σ_{i∈S} p_i v_i` lossless — the inference dual of Prop 2, feeding `riir-gpu` gather-GEMV.
- **Support-set fingerprint (stretch):** canonical (indices, values) sparse encoding of routing state → BLAKE3-committed shard latent field / KG triples ("head attends block") in riir-neuron-db.

### 2.5 Honest caveats

1. **γ=−0.5 is IID-Gaussian-optimal only.** Real heads are not IID Gaussian (the paper's own per-head γ varies in sign). The derived default must beat the fixed-α baseline first; the offline sweep is the bridge, not decoration.
2. **Quality anchors are the paper's models.** Our gates re-measure on our routing surfaces (Bench 032 harness pattern); the anchors set targets, not pass conditions.
3. **RoPE re-entry periodicity** forbids naive "drop everything past the first cutoff" eviction — retention must respect the window union.
4. **No quality-parity claim is made for pretrained models** — we do not swap softmax→entmax on trained weights (the paper's Copy-table result shows naive fixed-α conversion *hurts*; the retrofit path is Plan 396 Ph2 distillation, gated).

## TL;DR

oMLX PR #2756 adds a hybrid prompt-processing path for dense Qwen 3.5/3.6/3.8: eligible fixed-size (2048-token) prefill blocks split the **output channels** of MLP gate/up projections (and GDN `z+qkv`) across **both physical ANE instances + the GPU concurrently**, with a Metal merge kernel that fuses SwiGLU. |---|---|---|
| 1 | **Two instance-pinned ANE submissions per op** (not one unpinned) | Private runtime does not stripe one call across both ANEs: 55% MLP slice 57.90ms unpinned vs 41.51ms as two pinned calls |
| 2 | **Procedure banks** — 112 procedures (64 MLP + 48 GDN) packed into 2 resident programs (one per ANE) | Private runtime caps resident handles at ~121; one-program-per-layer would exhaust the budget before covering MLP+GDN |
| 3 | **Combined gate/up (and z/qkv) in one procedure** | Splitting into more calls raises launch count + sync overhead without adding useful ANE parallelism |
| 4 | **Merge kernel fuses SwiGLU** | Avoids full gate/up materialization + extra concatenations (our B15 fused-dual-GEMV+GeGLU shape) |
| 5 | **Blocking input-pack wait is intentional** | Async alternatives all measured worse; Metal-callback submission made a layer 47.5 → 71ms and the full body slower than GPU-only. Blocking lets ANE submit while the GPU suffix is still queued |
| 6 | **Eager compile at model load** (config ∈ runtime signature) | 112-procedure bank = 37-44s compile; moved out of first request |
| 7 | **Exact fixed-shape eligibility only, fail-open** — no synthetic padding | Qwen padding token is learned: padding perturbs logits, KV positions, RoPE, GDN recurrent state. Tails/decode/verify/unsupported → GPU |
| 8 | **INT8 per-output-channel ANE weights from resident q4; down-proj stays GPU** | Requant is not bit-exact (cosine 0.9992 / 0.9985, top-1 unchanged, HumanEval 28/30 both). An experimental split-down path had unacceptable errors and was **removed, not flagged** |

Residual inefficiency honestly reported: each ANE runs only ~38.8% duty cycle; downtime is dominated by waiting on upstream GPU dependency chains, not submission delay (tens of µs) — more submissions cannot fix it without restructuring the layer boundary.
